# 08. Data and Training Attacks

> [!info] Exam alignment
> **Domain:** 2.0 — Securing AI Systems (40%)
> **Objectives:** 2.6 (training-time attacks)
> **Source notes:** `../08. Data and Training Attacks/` (6 files)

## Overview

This module is the training-time half of objective 2.6 — the attacks that corrupt what a model *learns* rather than how it behaves at the prompt level. It covers the six-attack distractor set the exam loves to shuffle: **data poisoning, model poisoning, introducing biases, transfer learning attacks, model skewing, and backdoor/trojan attacks**. For every attack, know the four-part exam shape: what it is → how it works → what evidence it leaves → which compensating control is best. The distinctions between these six are classic exam material because their names sound similar but their targets, timing, and signatures differ.

## Data Poisoning

**Definition.** Tampering with the *training data* — injecting malicious or misleading information into the data used to teach the model, so the model learns wrong associations and makes attacker-influenced decisions.

**How it works.**
- Most exposed surfaces: public or user-supplied data collected for training, outsourced labeling, web-scraped corpora, and shared datasets.
- Common forms: mislabeled samples (label flipping), injected samples that associate a benign trigger with a desired output, and crafted "legitimate-looking" records that bias a classifier (e.g., feeding a fraud model fraud examples labeled as genuine).
- Even a small portion of poisoned training data can influence the model — you do not need to corrupt the whole dataset.
- Backdoors can be implanted this way (see [Backdoor and trojan attacks](#backdoor-and-trojan-attacks)); a poisoned corpus powering a RAG system is the retrieval-time sibling — [[05. RAG|Module 05]].

**Evidence/indicators.**
- Sudden or unexplained accuracy degradation, especially on specific classes.
- Anomalous or out-of-distribution samples discovered in the training set during audit.
- Provenance gaps — records whose origin cannot be traced.
- Post-deployment drift that training-data review should have caught.

**Compensating controls.** Data integrity controls are the anchor: validate and sanitize data sources, track data provenance, run anomaly detection over training sets, and control who can contribute data ([[04. Data Processing|Module 04]]). Pair with model evaluation after training ([[12. AI Security Controls|Module 12]]) and drift monitoring in operation ([[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]]).

## Model Poisoning

**Definition.** Attacking the *learned parameters of the model itself* (or the training process that produces them) so the model misbehaves **even when the training data looks clean**.

**How it works.**
- Attackers exploit that engineers reuse pre-trained models: they persuade developers to adopt a maliciously designed foundation model, gaining influence over every downstream system that relies on it.
- Malicious models are hosted on public repositories and model hubs, promoted with classic social-engineering and phishing techniques ("safe", "performant", look-alike names).
- The compromise is inherited silently — no suspicious data, just a suspicious artifact.

**Evidence/indicators.**
- A model in the stack whose origin/version is undocumented or unverifiable.
- Behavior the training data cannot explain (outputs skewing in a consistent, attacker-useful direction).
- Failed or missing integrity checks — no checksum/signature verification of model downloads.
- Reputation/red-flag signals around the model's source (typosquatted names, brand-new publisher accounts).

**Compensating controls.** Validate and use trusted foundation models before use; document origin and version; **treat the model as a security boundary**; verify integrity with checksums or signatures where available. This attack is also the model-artifact face of the broader [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|AI supply chain attack]] — signed models, vetted registries, and software composition analysis apply. Placement in the lifecycle: the model development/selection stage ([[06. Security in the AI Lifecycle|Module 06]]).

## Introducing Biases

**Definition.** The introduction of unfair, unbalanced, or harmful model outputs — either as an **intentional attack** or as an **unintended** consequence of development.

**How it works.**
- Intentional: an attacker skews training data or labels to push the model toward biased outcomes (a data-poisoning variant whose payload is unfairness rather than a trigger).
- Unintentional: unrepresentative datasets, poor sampling, or biased labeling introduce bias by accident — no attacker required.

**Evidence/indicators.**
- Disparate performance across demographic groups in evaluation or in production complaints.
- Fairness audit findings (demographic parity / equal-opportunity gaps).
- Outputs that systematically favor or disfavor a group on equivalent inputs.

**Compensating controls.** Representative and balanced datasets ([[04. Data Processing|Module 04]] data balancing), bias audits and fairness testing **before deployment** ([[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]]), and the governance gates of [[20. AI GRC|Module 20]]. Security relevance: biased models can be manipulated, produce discriminatory decisions, and erode trust.

## Transfer Learning Attacks

**Definition.** Attacks that ride the fine-tuning workflow: a fine-tuned model **inherits any hidden issues in the base model**, so a compromised foundation model carries its compromise forward through every legitimate fine-tune.

**How it works.**
- Transfer learning fine-tunes a pre-trained model for a new task — a valuable shortcut when data or compute is limited, which is exactly why it is popular.
- If an attacker compromises the foundation model (via model poisoning or a compromised distribution channel), fine-tuning does not launder the compromise: backdoors and skews survive.
- Transfer learning can also *amplify* inherited issues such as bias in the underlying model.

**Evidence/indicators.**
- A fine-tuned model misbehaving in ways its (clean, well-curated) fine-tuning data cannot explain.
- Base-model provenance that cannot be verified.
- Targeted testing (see backdoors below) finding trigger-activated behavior the fine-tune should have eliminated.

**Compensating controls.** Treat external models as **untrusted code**; verify provenance; evaluate fine-tuned models for hidden or backdoored behavior *after* fine-tuning, not just the base model before. Fine-tuning mechanics: [[02. Model Training Techniques|Module 02]]; evaluation tooling: [[12. AI Security Controls|Module 12]]; supply-chain distribution angle: [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]].

## Model Skewing

**Definition.** An intentional attempt to **distort a deployed AI system's behavior over time** by manipulating the data it consumes or the way people use it — the model's architecture and code are never touched.

**How it works.**
- Most effective against reinforcement-learning pipelines and any feedback loop (systems that update themselves from user interactions, ratings, or outcomes).
- The attacker **follows the normal processes users are expected to take** — submitting crafted feedback, interactions, or data through legitimate channels — which makes skewing difficult to detect.
- Example: repeatedly submitting crafted feedback so a recommendation engine or spam filter drifts toward the attacker's goal.

**Evidence/indicators.**
- Gradual behavioral drift that tracks an abnormal interaction pattern rather than any data or code change.
- Unusual feedback/usage patterns from specific accounts or clusters.
- Skew caught by periodic model review — not by accuracy checks on a single snapshot.

**Compensating controls.** Validate user input; **monitor feedback loops**; detect and correct skew through periodic model review ([[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]] — rate and usage monitoring). Rate limiting and access controls on who can feed the loop slow coordinated skewing campaigns.

## Backdoor and Trojan Attacks

**Definition.** A **backdoor** causes a model to respond in an attacker-controlled way **when a specific trigger is present**; a **trojan** embeds hidden malicious behavior in AI artifacts. The model works normally otherwise — which is the point.

**How it works.**
- Attack vectors: poisoned training data, poisoned model weights, and compromised supply chain — a backdoor is the *payload* those vectors can deliver.
- The trigger can be anything the model consumes: a rare token in text, a visual patch/sticker in an image, a specific phrase in a transaction.
- Example: a spam classifier that passes all mail containing a specific unrelated phrase; a vision model that misclassifies any image containing a small sticker.

**Evidence/indicators.**
- Sudden out-of-character responses **for specific inputs only** — broad accuracy checks stay green, which is why testing must hunt triggers, not just measure accuracy.
- Targeted/adversarial evaluation (e.g., trigger-search tooling — [[12. AI Security Controls|Module 12]] ART and Counterfit) revealing hidden behavior.

**Compensating controls.**
- Prevent the initial compromise: use trusted sources, verify downloads, treat model updates and third-party components as untrusted code.
- Test to reveal hidden behavior: look for inputs that cause sudden out-of-character responses rather than relying only on broad accuracy checks.
- Limit impact: add extra checks before the model performs high-impact actions (a human validation gate — [[07. Human-Centric AI Design|Module 07]]), and least privilege on what the model can do ([[13. AI Access Controls|Module 13]]).

## The Six Attacks Compared (mandatory table)

| Attack | What is corrupted | When it acts | Signature tell | First-line control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Data poisoning | Training **data** | Training time | Anomalous samples; class-specific accuracy drop | Data verification + provenance (data integrity controls) |
| Model poisoning | Model **artifact/process** | Adopted at model selection | Unverifiable origin; behavior data can't explain | Trusted sources, checksums/signatures, treat model as boundary |
| Introducing biases | Data or labels (skewed) | Training time | Disparate outcomes across groups | Balanced datasets, fairness audits pre-deployment |
| Transfer learning attack | **Base** model, inherited via fine-tune | Fine-tuning | Clean fine-tune data, inherited misbehavior | Vet base models; evaluate **after** fine-tuning |
| Model skewing | **Live feedback/usage** (no code/data attack) | Over time, in production | Drift tracking abnormal interaction patterns | Feedback-loop monitoring, periodic review |
| Backdoor/trojan | Payload delivered by any of the above | Dormant until **trigger** appears | Out-of-character responses on specific inputs only | Trigger-hunting evaluation; extra checks on high-impact actions |

> [!tip] The one-line separators
> Poisoning corrupts **what was learned**; skewing corrupts **what keeps being learned**; a backdoor is **dormant until triggered**; bias is the outcome, malicious or accidental.

## Exam Focus

- Objective 2.6 verbatim: "Given a scenario, analyze the evidence of an attack and suggest compensating controls for AI systems." These six attacks are the training-time entries on its attack list (the prompt-side entries are [[09. Prompt and Input Manipulation Attacks|Module 09]]; the extraction/integration entries are [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]] and [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
- Compare-and-contrast pairs to master:
  - **Data poisoning vs model poisoning** — corrupt data at rest/collection vs corrupt the model artifact or training process; model poisoning shows clean data and a dirty model.
  - **Poisoning vs skewing** — one-shot corruption at training time vs continuous manipulation of a *deployed* system through its legitimate feedback channels.
  - **Backdoor vs poisoning** — a backdoor is trigger-activated and dormant; poisoning degrades or steers generally.
  - **Intentional vs unintentional bias** — the objective says "introducing biases" under *attacks*, but the accidental variant is the distractor; both are mitigated with balancing and audits.
- Classic distractor confusions: picking "model skewing" for a corrupted dataset (that is data poisoning); picking "data poisoning" when the model hub was the vector (that is model poisoning / supply chain); confusing transfer learning attacks with model *theft* (extraction is [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]).
- Likely scenario shapes: an incident description → name the attack (look for the tells in the table above); an attack → pick the **best** compensating control (verification/provenance for poisoning; trusted-source integrity checks for model poisoning; feedback monitoring for skewing; trigger-hunting evaluation plus human gates for backdoors); "even a small fraction of poisoned data" → data poisoning's potency point.
- Map to OWASP ML Security Top 10 (Module 12): data poisoning ≈ ML02, model poisoning ≈ ML10, transfer learning attack ≈ ML07, model skewing ≈ ML08.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Data poisoning | Corrupting training data so the learned model is wrong or backdoored |
| Model poisoning | Malicious manipulation of the model artifact or training process; behavior misleads even with clean data |
| Introducing biases | Embedding systematic unfair outcomes — intentionally via skewed data/labels or accidentally via unrepresentative data |
| Transfer learning attack | Compromise inherited from a base model through fine-tuning |
| Model skewing | Distorting a deployed system's behavior over time via crafted feedback/data through legitimate channels |
| Backdoor | Trigger-activated attacker-controlled model behavior; model behaves normally otherwise |
| Trojan | Hidden malicious behavior embedded in an AI artifact |
| Label flipping | Poisoning form: changing sample labels to teach wrong associations |
| Provenance (data) | Traceable origin and history of data — the control that catches poisoned inputs |
| Data integrity controls | The compensating-control family for poisoning: verification, sanitization, provenance, anomaly detection |
| Trusted source / registry | Vetted origin for models and datasets; the control against model poisoning |
| Checksum/signature verification | Integrity proof for downloaded model artifacts |
| Adversarial robustness testing | Trigger/behavior hunting with tooling such as ART and Counterfit |
| Feedback loop | Self-updating data channel (ratings, interactions) — the skewing attack surface |
| Fairness testing | Pre-deployment evaluation for disparate outcomes — the bias control |
| Concept/behavioral drift | Model behavior shifting from its deployment baseline — the skewing evidence signal |

## Self-Check

1. A fraud model's accuracy holds steady, but auditors find that transactions containing one specific shipping note always pass. Training data review shows a handful of near-duplicate "legitimate" examples that no one remembers collecting. Which attack, and which control should have caught it?
2. A team downloads a popular foundation model from a new hub account to fine-tune a support classifier. The fine-tuning data is entirely their own, clean, and well-labeled — yet the deployed model intermittently exfiltrates customer text in responses. What happened, and what controls failed?
3. Over three months, a spam filter quietly starts letting through a particular marketing pattern after a burst of "not spam" feedback from a cluster of new accounts. No code or data changes occurred. Which attack is this?
4. Which attack on this module's list can occur with **no attacker at all**?
5. Why does broad accuracy testing fail to detect backdoors, and what does detect them?
6. An organization mandates: "all externally sourced models must be treated as untrusted code." Which two attacks does this primarily counter?
7. A loan-approval model performs well overall but denies one demographic at twice the rate of similarly qualified applicants, and the training set under-represents that group. Attack or accident — and what is the control set?
8. A colleague says: "Model skewing is just data poisoning with extra steps." What is the distinction an exam answer needs?

### Answers

1. **Data poisoning** delivering a **backdoor** (the shipping note is the trigger). Controls that should have caught it: data provenance tracking and anomaly detection over the training set, plus targeted trigger-hunting evaluation before deployment.
2. **Model poisoning via a poisoned foundation model, inherited through transfer learning** — the fine-tune launders nothing. Failed controls: trusted-source validation, provenance/version documentation, and checksum/signature verification of the artifact; post-fine-tune evaluation for hidden behavior.
3. **Model skewing** — behavior distorted over time through the legitimate feedback loop, no architecture or training-data compromise. Controls: feedback-loop monitoring, validation of user input, periodic model review.
4. **Introducing biases (the unintentional variant)** — unrepresentative data, poor sampling, or biased labeling can introduce bias with no adversary. (The exam lists it under attacks because the intentional variant exists.)
5. A backdoored model behaves normally on the general distribution — broad accuracy stays high. Detection requires targeted evaluation looking for inputs that trigger out-of-character responses, plus treating model updates as untrusted and adding checks before high-impact actions.
6. **Model poisoning and transfer learning attacks** (both deliver compromise through externally sourced artifacts); the same habit counters the model-distribution side of AI supply chain attacks ([[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
7. Accident (unrepresentative training data) rather than an attack — though the *evidence* is identical to intentional bias introduction. Control set: representative/balanced datasets, bias audits, and fairness testing before deployment, with monitoring for disparate outcomes.
8. Poisoning is a **training-time** corruption of the data or artifact — a snapshot compromise. Skewing is the **ongoing** manipulation of a deployed system through the data and feedback it consumes in normal operation, which is why periodic review (not a one-time audit) is the control.
