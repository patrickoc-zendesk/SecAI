# 06. Security in the AI Lifecycle

> [!info] Exam alignment
> **Domain:** 1.0 — Basic AI Concepts Related to Cybersecurity (17%)
> **Objectives:** 1.3
> **Source notes:** `../06. Security in the AI Lifecycle/` (8 files)

## Overview

Objective 1.3 asks you to explain the importance of security throughout the life cycle of AI — not merely to recite stage names. The AI life cycle is the sequence of stages a model moves through from initial planning to retirement, and it is cyclical: feedback from operations flows back into the next planning round. A weakness introduced at any stage (poisoned data, an unvetted pretrained model, a skipped evaluation) propagates into every later stage and every future iteration, so security must be embedded at every stage rather than bolted on before release — the security-by-design principle. Expect scenario questions that ask *when* a security decision should be made, *which stage* an attack targets, and *what control* belongs to a given stage. CompTIA also expects fluency in SDLC/MDLC vocabulary, because the AI life cycle extends classic software life-cycle security ideas with data-centric and model-centric stages.

## The AI Life Cycle and Security by Design

The AI life cycle describes the stages a model moves through from initial planning to retirement:

1. **Planning and business alignment** — define the objectives for the AI and how success will be measured.
2. **Data collection** — gather trustworthy, authentic, representative data.
3. **Data preparation** — clean, sanitize, and structure the data for modeling.
4. **Model selection and development** — choose the algorithm/architecture and train it.
5. **Model evaluation and validation** — measure performance and confirm behavior.
6. **Model deployment and integration** — release the model into production.
7. **Monitoring and maintenance** — watch for drift, misuse, and attack.
8. **Feedback and iteration** — feed results into the next planning round.

Two properties drive every exam question on this topic:

- **The life cycle is cyclical.** The feedback stage informs the next planning and business alignment stage. Models are retrained, tuned, and redeployed repeatedly — each iteration re-opens every security question, so iteration is where yesterday's secure model becomes tomorrow's vulnerable one.
- **Security should be embedded at every stage rather than added at the end (security by design).** This mirrors the "shift left" idea from DevSecOps: the earlier a flaw is caught, the cheaper it is to fix. For AI the stakes are higher than in classic software — a model trained on poisoned or biased data cannot simply be patched like an application; the defect is baked into the learned weights, so prevention at the early data stages is the primary control.

### SDLC and MDLC

Both acronyms appear on the exam's list:

- **SDLC (software development life cycle)** — the classic plan, design, build, test, deploy, maintain cycle for traditional software.
- **MDLC (machine learning development life cycle)** — the AI-specific life cycle above. The MDLC adds stages classic software lacks — data collection, data preparation, model evaluation, retraining loops — and with them a new attack surface: data poisoning, model drift, adversarial inputs, and model extraction. Security practices from the SDLC (secure design, testing, patching) carry over, but they must be extended with data- and model-specific controls.

### Stage-by-Stage Security View

| Stage | Security activities | Key controls | Attacks that target this stage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Business use case | Define security requirements, risk appetite, data classification, and compliance obligations | Risk assessment; KPIs and acceptable risk thresholds; system boundaries | Misaligned objectives; shadow AI (unapproved tools filling the gap) |
| Data collection | Vet sources; verify data is trustworthy and authentic; review provenance, bias, representativeness; minimize what is gathered | Data minimization; provenance records; source vetting; consent and licensing checks | Data poisoning via untrusted/unvetted sources |
| Data preparation | Clean, sanitize, validate, split, and augment data in a controlled environment | PII masking, tokenization, anonymization; version control and lineage; trusted tools and pipelines | PII leakage; train/test contamination; poisoned transformations |
| Model development/selection | Choose architecture; harden the training environment; vet pretrained models and dependencies | Firewalls; encryption; strong access control; trusted registries; checksums and signed artifacts | Backdoors and malicious code in pretrained models ([[11. | Model evaluation | Test on unseen data; measure accuracy, precision, recall, F1; detect overfitting | Cross-validation; regularization; hold-out test set | Evasion that exploits poor generalization |
| Deployment | Harden infrastructure; enforce access control; validate inputs/outputs; enable secure logging | Input validation; output filtering; least privilege; secure logging | Prompt injection and adversarial inputs ([[09. ## Business Use Case: Alignment with Corporate Objectives

The first stage focuses on defining what problem the new tool, feature, or model will solve, why it matters, and what success looks like. Teams define the AI business case and its expected value. Without this focus, the model may produce results that do not support the company's goals — and security effort spent protecting a misaligned system is wasted.

Why this stage matters most for security:

- **This is where security requirements are defined.** Deciding what the system must protect, against whom, and at what cost belongs here — before any data is collected or architecture chosen.
- **This is where risk appetite is set.** Risk assessment identifies what can go wrong if the system fails or is compromised; acceptable risk thresholds are agreed here, not discovered after an incident.
- **This is where success criteria (KPIs) are defined** — measurable targets for the AI's performance, including security metrics. Later evaluation and validation stages test against these; without them, "good enough" is undefined.
- **This is where system boundaries are drawn.** A clear understanding of the business helps define the system boundaries — what is in scope, what data flows in and out, what it is allowed to touch — which defines the attack surface to defend.
- **This is where compliance obligations are identified.** Which regulations and internal policies apply (finance, healthcare, privacy) determines constraints on every later stage, from what data may be collected to how outputs must be validated.

The exam loves "when should X be decided" questions — the answer pattern is almost always **the business use case / planning stage**, because requirements set at the start drive every downstream control. A practical corollary: when an organization has no fast, formal process for defining AI use cases, teams route around it with unapproved "shadow AI" tools — a governance risk that originates at this stage.

## Data Collection: Trustworthiness and Authenticity

High-quality data is the foundation of every AI system — the reliability of a model is determined by the quality, diversity, and trustworthiness of its data. During collection, data should be reviewed for accuracy, completeness, and authenticity, and checked for representativeness, provenance, bias, and fairness. Legal and ethical factors (consent, licensing, privacy obligations) are also crucial at this stage.

The mandatory distinction pair for this stage:

| Concept | What it means | How it is checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trustworthiness | The data and its source can be relied upon — sourced from reputable, vetted sources; accurate, complete, consistent, representative, and free of bias | Source vetting, quality profiling, bias and fairness review |
| Authenticity | The data is genuine — it is what it claims to be and has not been fabricated, altered, or spoofed in transit | Provenance records, chain of custody, checksums/hashes, comparison with authoritative sources |

Distractor pattern: "verifying the data has not been tampered with" = **authenticity**; "confirming the source is reputable and the data is representative and unbiased" = **trustworthiness**.

Security-relevant collection principles:

- **Data minimization** — only gather what is necessary to achieve the project's goals. Collecting extra data only increases cost and risk: more PII to protect, a larger breach surface, and broader compliance scope.
- **Diversity** — models trained on narrow examples perform poorly in new situations; non-representative data also produces unfair outcomes for underrepresented groups.
- **Source vetting** — untrusted or unvetted sources introduce the risk of **data poisoning**, where malicious samples corrupt the trained model. Provenance — a record of where data came from — is the control that makes later trust decisions possible. See [[08. Data and Training Attacks|Module 08]].

## Data Preparation

Also known as **preprocessing**, data preparation involves cleaning, organizing, and securing raw data so it can be used effectively in model development. It should happen in a secure and controlled environment using trusted tools and secure pipelines — the preprocessing stage handles the full dataset in concentrated form, so a compromised pipeline exposes everything at once.

The preparation steps:

1. **Problem framing and data profiling** — understand what data exists, its distributions, quality, and gaps before transforming anything.
2. **Data cleansing** — deduplication, fixing errors, handling missing data, and eliminating outliers that could distort results. Cleansing decisions should be recorded, not ad hoc.
3. **Data sanitization** — required for standards such as HIPAA and GDPR:
   - **Masking** — hiding sensitive parts of data (for example, replacing characters of a card number) so they are not exposed to the modeling pipeline.
   - **Tokenization** — replacing sensitive values with non-sensitive tokens, with a vault or mapping that lets authorized systems recover the original.
   - **Anonymization** — irreversibly removing identifying information so data can no longer be tied to an individual. Distinguish from **pseudonymization**, which swaps identifiers for pseudonyms but remains reversible with the mapping key — a classic exam distinction.
4. **Data validation and verification** — compare data against authoritative sources; check that values are accurate, complete, and properly formatted.
5. **Data splitting** — divide data into training, validation, and test sets. The test set must remain unseen so evaluation measures generalization; if test data leaks into training (contamination), measured performance is inflated and the model is weaker than reported.
6. **Data augmentation** — create synthetic examples or transform data to improve coverage, robustness, and class balance. Synthetic data still needs quality checks — augmentation that amplifies skewed patterns amplifies bias too.

Two practices wrap around all steps:

- **Version control and lineage tracking** — record how datasets evolve through successive cleaning and transformation steps. Traceability is critical for debugging, audits, and demonstrating compliance with internal and external data standards.
- **Quality assurance** remains the top priority throughout — a model built on unsound data inherits every defect, and no downstream control fully compensates for it. Sanitization techniques are covered in depth in [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]], and the data pipeline itself in [[04. Data Processing|Module 04]].

## Model Development and Selection

This stage begins with choosing the algorithm or architecture that best meets the project objectives; the selected model is then trained on the prepared data to learn patterns and guide decision making.

**Model choice is a security choice.** The right model impacts how vulnerable the feature will be:

- Simple models — decision trees, linear regression — are less vulnerable: fewer parameters, more interpretable, easier to reason about.
- Neural networks are more powerful but also more prone to being manipulated: large parameter counts, adversarial examples, and difficulty explaining decisions all enlarge the attack surface.

**Build vs. buy.** If a pretrained model is chosen, hidden backdoors or malicious code can be introduced through unverified providers — the model supply chain. Mitigate by sourcing models from trusted registries and verifying integrity (checksums, signed artifacts). A software bill of materials (SBOM) documents a system's components; for AI, an AI bill of materials (AI BOM) extends this to models, datasets, and dependencies so every component is inventoried and checkable. Supply-chain and third-party integration risks are covered in [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]].

**Training environments must be tightly controlled** using firewalls, encryption, and strong access control. Training data is a concentrated, high-value asset, and the training process itself (weights, checkpoints, training scripts) is an attack target — see [[08. Data and Training Attacks|Module 08]] and [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]].

## Model Evaluation and Validation

These are two distinct activities — a guaranteed exam distinction:

- **Evaluation measures how accurately a model performs.** The model is tested on unseen data, which helps determine **generalization** — how well learned patterns hold on data it has never seen. Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores are measured to gauge performance. A model performing well on training data but struggling with unseen data is likely **overfit** — it memorized the training set rather than learning general patterns. Overfitting is reduced with techniques such as cross-validation, regularization, and holding out a separate test set.
- **Validation confirms the model behaves ethically, securely, and within the organization's expectations.** It assesses whether the model meets fairness, security, and compliance standards, and can include red team exercises that attack the model before release. This is the model risk assessment view covered in [[12. AI Security Controls|Module 12]].

Note the objective's ordering: evaluation comes before deployment, and validation appears after it. Validation therefore has two faces — pre-release acceptance testing (does this model meet our fairness and security standards before we ship it?) and post-deployment verification (does the deployed system behave in production as it did in testing?). Ongoing, periodic validation continues through the monitoring stage and is a human-centric activity — see [[07. Human-Centric AI Design|Module 07]].

## Model Deployment and Integration

Deployment is where the model becomes a live, attackable system. Deployment considerations include:

- **Hardening deployment infrastructure** — least privilege, patching, container/host hardening, network segmentation of inference endpoints.
- **Enforcing access control** — who may call the model, who may update it, who may read its outputs. See [[13. AI Access Controls|Module 13]].
- **Protecting the model asset** — guards against model theft and unauthorized extraction of weights. A stolen model can be studied offline, cloned, or repackaged — see [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]].
- **Validating inputs and outputs (secure integration)** — input validation and output filtering defend against prompt injection and adversarial inputs from users and from integrated systems. See [[09. Prompt and Input Manipulation Attacks|Module 09]] and [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]].
- **Enabling secure logging** — audit trails for accountability and incident response, while avoiding logging sensitive prompts, PII, or secrets that pass through the system.

## Monitoring and Maintenance

Monitoring delivers two benefits: detecting drift, and detecting unexpected behavior or potential attacks. Both are covered operationally in [[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]].

The mandatory drift distinction pair:

- **Data drift** — the data entering the model changes from what it was trained on (for example, a change in customer buying habits). The world changed; the model did not.
- **Model drift** — the model's internal parameters or assumptions degrade as the environment or the behavior it predicts evolves. Even with unchanged inputs, the relationship the model learned stops holding (often called concept drift when the input-to-output relationship itself shifts).

Distractor pattern: "customer behavior changed after a market shift, so predictions are worse" = **data drift**; "the model's predictions slowly degrade as assumptions stop holding" = **model drift**.

Maintenance activities:

- **Security patching and library updates are critical** — the framework and dependency stack an AI system runs on carries the same CVE risk as any other software.
- **Retraining** — rebuilding the model with new, verified data to correct drift or address emerging risks. Costly, and it restarts the evaluation and validation stages — but sometimes nothing less fixes the problem.
- **Tuning** — small adjustments such as refining thresholds or hyperparameters to optimize performance without requiring a rebuild. Tuning is the fast, cheap response; retraining is the thorough one.

A key security rule: retraining must use **verified** data — otherwise the maintenance stage itself becomes a poisoning vector.

## Feedback and Iteration

The notes cover this stage in the lifecycle overview but not in detail — this section fills the gap, because the objective lists it explicitly and the exam tests it.

The life cycle is cyclical: feedback informs the next planning and business alignment round. Feedback sources include user interactions, incident findings, monitoring and drift signals, and evaluation results. Iteration is normal — most deployed models are retrained or tuned many times.

The security risk specific to this stage: **retraining on user feedback introduces poisoning risk**. If user feedback, user-submitted data, or interaction logs flow into retraining without vetting, an attacker can submit manipulated feedback or samples to steer the next model version — feedback-loop poisoning. Systems tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback are exposed on the preference data too. See [[08. Data and Training Attacks|Module 08]].

Controls that keep iteration safe:

- Treat user-contributed feedback and data as untrusted input — validate and vet it before it enters any retraining pipeline.
- Re-run evaluation and validation on every new model version (security and performance regression testing), not just the first release.
- Version models and datasets so any regression can be traced and rolled back.
- Route high-risk changes through human approval and change management — see [[07. Human-Centric AI Design|Module 07]].

## Exam Focus

- **Exact objective wording:** "Explain the importance of security throughout the life cycle of AI." Expect scenario framing: a described company, system, or incident — pick the stage, control, or concept.
- **Compare-and-contrast pairs to master:**
  - Trustworthiness (reliable, vetted source) vs. authenticity (genuine, unaltered data)
  - Evaluation (performance on unseen data) vs. validation (behavior, fairness, security, compliance)
  - Data drift (inputs change) vs. model drift (model's assumptions degrade)
  - Retraining (rebuild with verified data) vs. tuning (adjust thresholds/hyperparameters)
  - Build vs. buy (in-house training vs. pretrained model supply-chain risk)
  - SDLC vs. MDLC
  - Masking vs. tokenization vs. anonymization vs. pseudonymization
  - Security by design vs. bolt-on security
- **Classic distractor confusions:**
  - "Security requirements and risk thresholds are best defined during evaluation/deployment" — wrong; they belong to the business use case stage.
  - Data poisoning placed at the deployment or monitoring stage — it targets data collection/preparation, and the feedback/retraining loop.
  - Red teaming and fairness testing labeled "evaluation" — they are validation activities.
  - Tuning described as requiring a rebuild, or retraining described as a threshold change.
  - Authenticity and trustworthiness swapped (tamper-checking = authenticity; source reliability and representativeness = trustworthiness).
  - Pseudonymization presented as irreversible — only anonymization is.
- **Likely scenario shapes:**
  - "A company deploys an AI loan-approval system" — at which stage should risk thresholds, data classification, and compliance obligations have been set? (Business use case.)
  - "Which life-cycle stage does data poisoning primarily target?" (Data collection — also preparation and feedback/retraining.)
  - "A pretrained model from a public registry contains a backdoor" — which stage control failed, and what should have been done? (Trusted registries, checksums, signed artifacts.)
  - "Performance drops after customer habits change post-recession" — identify drift type and the right maintenance response.
  - "Team wants to retrain on user-submitted feedback" — name the risk and the control.

## Self-Check

1. A financial services firm is scoping a new AI fraud-scoring system. At which life-cycle stage should security requirements, data classification, and acceptable risk thresholds be defined?
   - A. Data collection
   - B. Business use case
   - C. Model evaluation
   - D. Monitoring and maintenance
2. A team verifies that a training dataset matches hashes recorded at the source and has a complete provenance chain, confirming the data has not been fabricated or altered. Which property have they established?
   - A. Trustworthiness
   - B. Representativeness
   - C. Authenticity
   - D. Diversity
3. Which life-cycle stage does data poisoning most directly target?
   - A. Data collection
   - B. Deployment
   - C. Monitoring
   - D. Feedback only
4. A model scores 99% accuracy on its training data but 61% on the held-out test set. What is the likely diagnosis and one standard corrective technique?
   - A. Data drift; retraining
   - B. Overfitting; cross-validation
   - C. Model drift; tuning
   - D. Data poisoning; sanitization
5. A startup downloads a pretrained model from an unverified public registry and later finds it contains a hidden backdoor. Which controls would have addressed this?
   - A. Input validation and output filtering
   - B. Trusted registries plus integrity verification (checksums, signed artifacts)
   - C. Data minimization and masking
   - D. Secure logging and dashboards
6. A retail recommendation model's accuracy declines after a recession permanently changes customer buying habits. The model and pipeline are unchanged. What is happening?
   - A. Model drift
   - B. Data drift
   - C. Overfitting
   - D. Backdoor activation
7. A team wants to improve model confidence thresholds without rebuilding it. Which maintenance activity fits?
   - A. Retraining
   - B. Tuning
   - C. Redeployment
   - D. Re-collection
8. After launch, a chatbot vendor plans to retrain monthly on raw user conversations. What is the primary security risk?
   - A. Model drift
   - B. Feedback-loop poisoning
   - C. Data minimization violations only
   - D. Overfitting

### Answers

1. **B** — Business use case. Requirements, risk appetite, data classification, KPIs, and compliance scope are set at planning; every later stage inherits them.
2. **C** — Authenticity: the data is genuine and unaltered. Trustworthiness (A) covers source reliability, accuracy, and representativeness.
3. **A** — Data collection (also preparation and the feedback/retraining loop). Poisoning corrupts the model through malicious samples; it does not happen at deployment or monitoring.
4. **B** — The gap between training and test performance indicates overfitting; cross-validation (or regularization, or a stricter hold-out) is the standard correction.
5. **B** — Pretrained model supply-chain risk: source from trusted registries and verify integrity with checksums and signed artifacts.
6. **B** — Data drift: the inputs (customer behavior) changed relative to the training distribution while the model itself is unchanged.
7. **B** — Tuning adjusts thresholds or hyperparameters to optimize performance without a rebuild.
8. **B** — Retraining on unvetted user feedback lets attackers steer the next model version — feedback-loop poisoning. Vet feedback data before it enters retraining (privacy concerns are real but not the primary poisoning risk).
