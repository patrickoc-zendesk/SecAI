# 15. AI Monitoring and Auditing

> [!info] Exam alignment
> **Domain:** 2.0 — Securing AI Systems (40%)
> **Objectives:** 2.5 — Given a scenario, implement monitoring and auditing for AI systems
> **Source notes:** `../15. AI Monitoring and Auditing/` (7 files)

## Overview

Objective 2.5 covers the operational half of securing AI systems: once controls like encryption ([[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]]) and access management ([[13. AI Access Controls|Module 13]]) are in place, monitoring and auditing are how you prove they work and detect when they fail. The objective has two halves — **monitoring** (prompts, logs, confidence, rates, costs) and **auditing** for quality and compliance (hallucinations, accuracy, bias and fairness, access). Because the exam is scenario-based, the core skill is matching: given an attack or a compliance requirement, pick the monitoring control that detects it or the audit that proves it. Every monitoring control here ties back to a specific attack from [[09. Prompt and Input Manipulation Attacks|Module 09]], [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]], and [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]].

## Prompt monitoring (query and response)

Prompt monitoring involves capturing and reviewing **both** the inputs that users send to the model and the outputs it generates. It helps detect unusual activity, prevent abuse, and maintain visibility into how people interact with the system. The objective splits it into two sides, and scenarios often name one side explicitly.

### Query-side monitoring

- Watch what is being **asked**: capture and review the prompts users submit before and as they reach the model.
- Primary detection target: **prompt injection** and related input-manipulation attacks — monitoring is how these get identified in production traffic (see [[09. Prompt and Input Manipulation Attacks|Module 09]]).
- Other query-side signals:
  - Unusual or anomalous prompt patterns (sudden change in style, topic, or volume for a user or tenant).
  - Attempts to extract system prompts, training data, or other users' content (extraction probing — see [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]).
  - Abuse of the service itself (scraping, jailbreak attempts, prohibited use cases).
- Query telemetry can be fed into a **SIEM** so AI traffic is correlated with the rest of the security estate — larger attack patterns that look harmless one request at a time become visible in aggregate.

### Response-side monitoring

- Watch what the model **answers**: review generated outputs for accuracy, safety, and compliance.
- Detection targets on the response side:
  - **PII or sensitive data leaking** into outputs (memorized training data or retrieved documents — see [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]).
  - Unsafe, toxic, or non-compliant content reaching users.
  - Hallucination indicators and quality degradation (confidence signals — next section).
- One caution that is also exam material: **capture logs must themselves be protected**, because prompts and responses can contain sensitive or personal data. Monitoring creates a new sensitive data store; treat it like one.

## Response confidence level

- Confidence levels indicate **how certain the model is about its answers** — response monitoring includes tracking them.
- Confidence is a quality and drift signal, not a guarantee:
  - Tracking confidence over time helps **identify drift or loss of accuracy** in the model.
  - If **confidence is high but accuracy is low**, the model is miscalibrated and **should be recalibrated**.
- Operationally, low-confidence responses can be routed to human review or fallback flows — a common guardrail pattern for high-stakes outputs.
- Exam trap: high confidence is **not** proof of correctness. A model can be confidently wrong; miscalibration (high confidence, low accuracy) is itself a finding that demands recalibration. Confidence measures the model's certainty, accuracy measures its correctness, and the two must be monitored together.

## Log monitoring

- Log monitoring **expands visibility by collecting and analyzing logs from every component that supports the AI service** — not just the model: API gateways, model servers, vector stores and retrieval pipelines, tool/plugin invocations, authentication and admin actions.
- What to log in an AI system:
  - **Prompts and responses** (the query/response telemetry above).
  - **Tool use** — which tools, plugins, or external functions the model invoked, and with what arguments (attack surface covered in [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
  - **Errors and failures** — failed generations, refusals, timeouts, plugin errors.
  - **Anomalies** — unexpected patterns in any of the above.
  - **Access events** — logins, permission changes, dataset and model updates (these feed the access audit trails below).
- Log reviews serve two purposes: verifying **system performance** and verifying the **effectiveness of security controls** — logs are the evidence that controls actually fired.
- Most organizations rely on **centralized platforms**:
  - **SIEM** (Security Information and Event Management) — **aggregates and correlates** logs.
  - **XDR** (Extended Detection and Response) — **adds automated cross-layer detection and response** on top of aggregation.
- Key practices:
  - **Uniform log formatting** — every component emits consistent, parseable logs, or correlation fails.
  - **Log sanitization** and **log protection** (the mandatory distinction pair — next section).
  - **External audit logging services** — logs are protected **even in the event of a compromise** of the primary system, because they live out of band.
  - **Clear log retention policies** — keep logs long enough for investigation and compliance, delete them on schedule (retention limits are themselves a privacy control — see [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]] minimization).

## Log sanitization vs. log protection

The objective lists these as two separate items, and swapping them is a classic distractor. **Sanitization is about what is in the logs; protection is about the logs themselves.**

### Log sanitization

- AI logs are uniquely dangerous as a data store: they can contain **full prompts, full responses, PII, and secrets** (API keys, tokens, credentials that flowed through the system).
- Sanitization means scrubbing that sensitive content **before logs are stored or shared** — redacting PII, masking secrets, tokenizing identifiers (the techniques from [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]] applied to the log stream).
- Driver: privacy and compliance. Unsanitized logs turn your monitoring infrastructure into an unmanaged sensitive-data repository — a breach of the logs is a breach of the data.
- Sanitization must not destroy the signal: what is removed has to be balanced against what detection still needs.

### Log protection

- Protection means keeping the logs **trustworthy and available as evidence**:
  - **Integrity / tamper-proofing** — logs cannot be altered or deleted by an attacker covering their tracks (hashing, signatures, append-only or write-once storage).
  - **Access control** — who can read or manage logs follows least privilege; audit logs are exactly what an intruder wants to rewrite.
  - **Retention** — logs survive long enough to investigate incidents and satisfy regulators, then are deleted per policy.
- **External audit logging services** are the strongest form: if the primary system is compromised, its out-of-band copies of the logs remain intact — the compromise cannot erase its own record.

### The distinction in one line

| | Log sanitization | Log protection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question answered | What sensitive content is in the logs? | Can the logs be trusted and kept? |
| Concern | Privacy / compliance exposure | Integrity, access, retention, availability |
| Techniques | Redact, mask, tokenize before storage/sharing | Tamper-proofing, access control, out-of-band storage, retention policy |

## Rate monitoring

- Rate monitoring tracks **how often key events occur in an AI system over time** — requests per user or tenant, tool invocations, generation volume, error rates.
- It serves three purposes:
  - **Detects security incidents** — sudden spikes in request rate are the signature of abuse: automated scraping, a DoS attempt against the model service, or high-volume systematic querying characteristic of **model extraction attacks** ([[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]).
  - **Identifies bugs** — runaway loops, retry storms, and malfunctioning integrations show up as rate anomalies.
  - **Maintains fairness in shared environments** — in multi-tenant systems, rate monitoring identifies tenants or users monopolizing shared capacity, and identifies areas where **throttling and rate limiting** should apply.
- Rate monitoring **enables adaptive rate limiting** — limits that respond to observed behavior rather than sitting static.
- Rate limiting itself is an access/enforcement control covered in [[13. AI Access Controls|Module 13]]; rate **monitoring** is the detection side that tells you where and how to apply it.

## AI cost monitoring

Cost monitoring safeguards financial resources by tracking usage and related costs. The objective breaks it into four sub-items, and note the mapping: models bill on **input and output tokens**, so prompt costs and response costs are separate line items.

| Objective sub-item | What it tracks |
| --- | --- |
| **Prompts** | Input token costs — what users send into the model |
| **Response** | Output token costs — what the model generates back |
| **Storage** | Memory, disk space, and input/output performance — datasets, checkpoints, vector stores, log growth |
| **Processing** | Compute costs — training runs, inference time, and third-party API costs for external models or services |

- Supporting mechanisms: **budget alerts and spending limits** — set thresholds so cost anomalies page a human before the budget is gone.
- **Cost anomalies are an attack signal, not just a finance problem.** Sudden spikes in rate or cost can signal abuse such as **denial-of-wallet attacks**, where an attacker drives up token or API spend to exhaust budgets — the AI analog of resource-exhaustion DoS against the model service (see [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
- Rate and cost spikes usually appear **together**: the same flood of expensive generation requests shows up in both monitors. In scenarios, an unexplained cost spike with normal-looking traffic can indicate a compromised credential being used programmatically.

## Auditing for quality and compliance

The second half of objective 2.5. Auditing confirms that safeguards are both **present and effective** — that the AI system generates accurate, trustworthy, verifiable information and meets established standards for reliability, compliance, and security. Four audit areas, each with its own method; compliance frameworks and documentation duties are covered in [[20. AI GRC|Module 20]].

### Hallucinations

- A hallucination occurs when a model **generates false information and presents it as true** — the defining quality failure of generative AI.
- Audit method: auditors **compute hallucination rates by comparing sample outputs to trusted sources** — a measurable rate, not a vibe.
- Ways to reduce hallucinations (also strong scenario answers):
  - **Require citations for important claims** — unsupported assertions stand out.
  - **Use RAG** to ground responses in verified source data (see [[05. RAG|Module 05]]).
  - **Leverage automated detection tools** for scale.
  - **Human verification** for high-stakes content.

### Accuracy

- Accuracy auditing assesses whether outputs are **correct and consistent for the tasks the system performs**.
- **The distinction from hallucination auditing is exam material:** hallucination auditing deals with *fabricated* information; accuracy auditing deals with *partial errors, misinterpretations, or incomplete answers* — an output can be non-hallucinated and still inaccurate.
- Core audit steps:
  1. **Define clear metrics based on the system's function** — there is no single accuracy number:
     - Classification model → proportion of correct predictions across test cases.
     - Chatbot → how often issues are resolved correctly, as determined by human reviewers.
     - Recommendation system → how relevant suggested items are to user needs.
  2. **Systematic testing against evaluation data** — test on a fixed evaluation set.
  3. **Factual validation** — validate facts and sources; verify citations against credible evidence.
  4. **Consistency and completeness** — correct answers must address the **full scope** of the request, not just part of it.
  5. **Incorporate user feedback** as a real-world accuracy signal.
- A **gradual decline in accuracy on the same dataset indicates concept drift** — real-world patterns change and the model's assumptions become outdated. The fix is **retraining or recalibration** to restore reliability.
- Confidence data from monitoring feeds this audit: the high-confidence/low-accuracy combination (Module 15, response confidence level) is the classic recalibration trigger.

### Bias and fairness

- **Bias** occurs when an AI system produces unfair differences in outcomes among users; **fairness** means decisions are consistent, just, and free from discrimination.
- Bias originates from two sources — a mandatory contrast pair:
  - **Data bias** — arises from training data that reflects social imbalance or stereotypes.
  - **Algorithmic bias** — results from the way models process data or assign weight to features.
- Bias audit steps:
  1. **Establish clear criteria** using statistical fairness measures:
     - **Demographic parity** — do different groups receive similar results?
     - **Equal opportunity** — do equally qualified individuals have the same chance of success?
     - **Equalized odds** — are error rates consistent across groups?
     - These three are the classic swap-and-confuse distractor set; know what each compares.
  2. **Test across subgroups** — both the data and the model outputs, across relevant subgroups, to find outcome differences that indicate fairness issues.
  3. **Test across model types and modalities** — the same bias can appear differently across models and output types.
  4. **Implement proactive fairness measures** — diverse and representative training data, fairness-aware algorithms, adjusting loss functions to encourage balanced outcomes.
  5. **Continuous monitoring after deployment**, recalibrating as data and conditions change — bias auditing is not one-and-done.
- **Incorporate diverse perspectives** into the audit itself.
- Findings and mitigation steps must be **documented for compliance** with regulations such as equal employment opportunity and fair lending laws (see [[20. AI GRC|Module 20]] for the framework side).

### Access

- Access auditing confirms that **access controls, authentication methods, and data protection measures work as intended**, and that **every system action can be traced to an authorized user**.
- Practices:
  - **Establish clear governance** — policies specifying who can access AI systems, how access is approved, and when it should be revoked. This establishes accountability and ensures permissions are reviewed regularly.
  - **Audit access controls** — follow **least privilege** and ensure critical tasks are separated among multiple roles: **no individual should have full authority to both initiate and approve sensitive actions** (separation of duties). Access-control mechanics are in [[13. AI Access Controls|Module 13]].
  - **Maintain audit trails** — record major actions (logins, access, updates) and ensure they **map correctly to users**. An audit trail that cannot attribute actions to identities is not an audit trail.
  - **Verify regulatory compliance** — GDPR, for example, requires detailed records of data access, including **timestamps, user IDs, and the purpose of each action**.
  - **Evaluate data protection measures** — review logs, models, and sensitive datasets to confirm encryption **at rest and in transit**, **tamper protection**, and **key rotation** (the controls from [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]]).
  - **Monitor third-party access** — vendors and integrations touching the AI system are inside the audit scope too.

## Exam Focus

- **Exact objective wording:** "Given a scenario, implement monitoring and auditing for AI systems." Sub-items: prompt monitoring (query; response); log monitoring; log sanitization; log protection; response confidence level; rate monitoring; AI cost monitoring (prompts; storage; response; processing); auditing for quality and compliance (hallucinations; accuracy; bias and fairness; access).
- **Compare-and-contrast pairs to master:**
  - **Log sanitization vs log protection** — content scrubbing (privacy) vs integrity/access/retention (evidence preservation). The single most likely swap on this objective.
  - **Query-side vs response-side prompt monitoring** — what users ask vs what the model answers.
  - **Rate monitoring vs cost monitoring** — event frequency vs financial spend (they spike together, but detect different outcomes).
  - **SIEM vs XDR** — aggregate and correlate vs add automated cross-layer detection and response.
  - **Hallucination audit vs accuracy audit** — fabricated content vs partial errors, misinterpretations, incompleteness.
  - **Data bias vs algorithmic bias** — training data reflects imbalance vs model processing/feature weighting.
  - **Demographic parity vs equal opportunity vs equalized odds** — similar results across groups vs same success chance for the equally qualified vs consistent error rates across groups.
  - **Confidence vs accuracy** — the model's certainty vs its correctness; miscalibration = high confidence with low accuracy → recalibrate.
- **Classic distractor confusions:**
  - Treating high confidence as proof of a correct answer.
  - Presenting hallucination reduction (RAG, citations) as the fix for a *drift-driven accuracy* problem (that needs retraining/recalibration).
  - Offering log *protection* (tamper-proofing) when the scenario is about PII *inside* the logs — that is sanitization.
  - Swapping equal opportunity and equalized odds in a fairness-metric scenario.
  - Naming rate *limiting* (an enforcement control) when the question asks for the *monitoring/detection* control.
- **Likely scenario shapes — which control detects which attack:**
  - Prompt injection in production traffic → query-side prompt monitoring, correlated in a SIEM ([[09. Prompt and Input Manipulation Attacks|Module 09]]).
  - High-volume systematic querying to reconstruct a model → rate monitoring ([[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]).
  - Denial-of-wallet / resource-exhaustion against the model service → cost monitoring spikes (rate spike alongside) ([[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
  - PII or memorized data appearing in outputs → response-side monitoring.
  - Gradual quality decay on a fixed test set → accuracy audit detecting concept drift; high confidence + low accuracy → recalibration.
  - Regulatory demand for proof of who accessed what data, when, and why → access audit trails (timestamps, user IDs, purpose) under GDPR-style requirements.

## Self-Check

1. A security team notices one account submitting thousands of rapid, near-identical queries with slight phrasing variations. Which monitoring control flags this, and what attack is it most consistent with?
2. Overnight, token spend triples with no new legitimate users. The budget alert fires on output-token costs. Which two monitoring domains produced this signal, and what attack pattern does it suggest?
3. Legal requires that stored AI logs never contain customer PII; operations requires that no administrator can alter past logs. Which control satisfies each requirement?
4. A model's accuracy on its fixed evaluation set has declined gradually over three months, while its confidence scores have stayed high. What is the diagnosis and the fix?
5. An auditor samples 500 model outputs and checks each against trusted reference sources. Which audit is being performed, and how does it differ from the accuracy audit?
6. A fairness audit finds that the model's error rate for one user group is double the others, while positive-outcome rates look similar across groups. Which fairness criterion is violated, and how does it differ from demographic parity?
7. Under a GDPR-style access audit, what three details must records of data access include, and what control guarantees they map to real identities?
8. What is the difference between what a SIEM and an XDR platform contribute to AI log monitoring?

### Answers

1. **Rate monitoring** — the spike in query frequency is its core signal. High-volume systematic querying with systematic phrasing variation is the signature of **model extraction** (reconstructing model behavior — see [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]); rate monitoring also feeds adaptive rate limiting to throttle it.
2. **Cost monitoring** (the budget alert on output/response costs) corroborated by **rate monitoring** (the traffic spike). The pattern suggests a **denial-of-wallet attack** — driving up token spend to exhaust budgets — the AI analog of resource-exhaustion DoS against the model service (see [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
3. **Log sanitization** satisfies legal — PII is scrubbed from logs before storage. **Log protection** satisfies operations — tamper-proofing, access control, retention, and out-of-band (external audit logging) storage keep logs unalterable even if the primary system is compromised. Sanitization governs log content; protection governs the logs themselves.
4. Gradual decline on a fixed evaluation set = **concept drift**; sustained high confidence with declining accuracy = **miscalibration**. The fix is **recalibration**, and if drift is the root cause, **retraining** on current data.
5. The **hallucination audit** — computing a hallucination rate by comparing sample outputs to trusted sources. The accuracy audit differs in scope: it covers partial errors, misinterpretations, and incomplete answers using task-appropriate metrics (proportion of correct predictions, resolution rates, relevance), not just fabricated content.
6. **Equalized odds** — it requires consistent error rates across groups; a doubled error rate violates it directly. Demographic parity only asks whether groups receive similar **results** (positive-outcome rates), which can hold even while error rates diverge.
7. **Timestamps, user IDs, and the purpose of each action.** The supporting control is the **audit trail** — records of logins, access, and updates that map correctly to users — backed by governance policies and least-privilege access reviews.
8. A **SIEM aggregates and correlates logs**; an **XDR** adds **automated cross-layer detection and response** on top of that aggregation and correlation.
