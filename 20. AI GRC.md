# 20. AI GRC

> [!info] Exam alignment
> **Domain:** 4.0 — AI Governance, Risk, and Compliance (19%)
> **Objectives:** 4.1, 4.2, 4.3
> **Source notes:** `../20. AI GRC/` (3 files)

## Overview

Domain 4.0 is the governance side of SecAI+: how an organization structures itself to use AI responsibly, the risks AI introduces beyond classic technical security, and the compliance landscape that shapes business use and development of AI. At 19% of the exam (roughly 11 of the 60 questions), it is the third-largest domain, and beta-exam feedback flagged it as the most surprising: questions are scenario-driven and test practical application, not just naming frameworks. The core exam skill is distinguishing binding law (the EU AI Act) from voluntary frameworks (NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and 23894) and intergovernmental principles (OECD) — and knowing which one actually obligates an organization in a given scenario. The three objectives form a chain: governance structures (4.1) exist to manage AI risks (4.2) and satisfy compliance obligations (4.3). For the technical counterpart to many governance requirements, see [[06. Security in the AI Lifecycle|Module 06]], [[12. AI Security Controls|Module 12]], and [[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]].

## 4.1 — Organizational Governance Structures That Support AI

Objective 4.1: "Explain organizational governance structures that support AI."

### What AI governance is and why it exists

- AI governance sets the rules around AI usage — who may build and deploy AI, for what purposes, with what data, and under which controls.
- It protects people and data: it reduces bias and leakage, improves reliability, and prevents surprises (unexpected model behavior, unapproved use cases).
- It supports explainability: documented decisions and model records mean the organization can answer "why did this system do that?"
- It provides a record — an audit trail of who approved what, which is what regulators, auditors, and courts will ask for.
- Governance spans the full AI lifecycle: design, development, deployment, and monitoring. It is not a one-time review at launch; it is continuous.

### Organizational structures

Organizational structures formalize decision-making authority. Without them, every team improvises its own AI rules and the organization cannot answer basic questions: who approved this model, who owns this risk, who can shut this system down?

#### AI Center of Excellence (CoE)

- A central team that concentrates AI expertise and manages the organization's AI portfolio.
- Typical responsibilities:
  - Sets standards and reusable patterns for AI development (model selection, evaluation, documentation).
  - Reviews and approves high-impact or novel AI use cases before they ship.
  - Provides a home for scarce AI skills that individual business units cannot each afford.
  - Curates standard components, security controls, and approved workflows that other teams consume.
  - Tracks the organization's AI inventory — the system of record for what AI exists where.

#### Hub-and-spoke governance model

Many organizations start with an AI CoE and adopt a hub-and-spoke model for AI adoption:

- The CoE is the hub.
- The product and business units act as spokes that execute delivery.
- The spokes build and run their own solutions but rely on the hub for standard components, security controls, and approved workflows.
- Benefit: delivery speed and domain context stay in the business units, while consistency and control stay central.
- Risk to watch: the hub can become a bottleneck, which drives teams toward shadow AI (see 4.2 below).

#### AI handoffs and decision authority

Governance is exercised through defined handoffs between:

- **Product owners** — define the use case, intended purpose, and acceptable risk for an AI feature.
- **Engineering teams** — build, test, and run the solution within the standards set by policy.
- **Risk, security, legal** — review and sign off on risk, controls, privacy, and regulatory exposure.
- **Executive sponsors** — accept residual risk and own accountability for consequential decisions.

The intended purpose recorded at handoff matters for compliance: under the EU AI Act, an AI system's risk tier is determined largely by its intended purpose, so a vague purpose statement becomes a compliance failure later.

#### AI policies and procedures

AI policies and procedures provide a common language for control and clarity across the organization. A policy states intent, creates authority, and sets scope. Mature organizations build a layered framework:

- **Policies** — set non-negotiable rules and intent for the whole organization. They state what is permitted or prohibited, who it applies to, and who is accountable. Example: "Customer data must not be sent to public AI services without an approved contract."
- **Standards** — convert policies into specific, testable control statements that set the minimum bar across teams. Example: "All production models must be registered in the AI inventory with an owner, purpose, and evaluation report."
- **Procedures** — describe how people complete a task step by step so that results stay consistent across teams and time. Example: the model-approval procedure, the incident-response procedure for AI data leakage.
- **Guidelines** — offer recommended practices that help teams make good choices where a single rule would be too rigid. Example: prompt-injection hardening guidance for teams building RAG assistants.

The layering is the point: policies are stable and few, standards make them testable, procedures make them repeatable, and guidelines handle the cases where judgment is needed.

#### Core AI policies an organization needs

- **AI acceptable use policy (AUP)** — what employees may and may not do with AI tools (which services, which data classes, disclosure of AI use, review requirements for AI-generated output).
- **Model approval / AI lifecycle policy** — how a model or AI feature moves from proposal to production: risk tiering, review gates, sign-off, and decommissioning.
- **Data handling policy for AI** — which data classifications may be used for training or prompting, minimization, anonymization, retention, and consent. Ties directly to [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]] (protecting data at rest and in use) and [[04. Data Processing|Module 04]] (preparing and sanitizing training data).
- **Monitoring and audit policy** — continuous monitoring, drift detection, and audit logging so governance claims are backed by evidence. See [[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]].
- **Third-party AI policy** — rules for buying, embedding, or licensing AI from vendors; evidence requirements before a vendor tool is sanctioned.
- **Incident response policy for AI** — who responds when a model leaks data, is jailbroken, hallucinates in a consequential setting, or is manipulated.

### AI-related roles

The objective lists ten roles. The exam's favorite trick in this domain is swapping roles in scenario answers, so the distinctions matter as much as the titles.

| Role | One-line responsibility | Owns in the AI lifecycle |
|---|---|---|
| Data engineer | Builds and maintains the pipelines that move, store, and prepare data | Data infrastructure, ingestion, storage, and data quality (everything before the model) |
| Data scientist | Analyzes data and runs experiments to answer business questions | Analysis, feature engineering, model selection and offline evaluation |
| Machine learning (ML) engineer | Turns an experimental model into production-quality serving code | Model build, training pipelines, and the serving/inference path |
| Platform engineer | Builds and maintains the underlying compute and environments | Infrastructure: capacity, environments, and tooling the AI workload runs on |
| MLOps engineer | Automates the ML lifecycle end to end | Deployment pipelines, monitoring, retraining triggers, and rollback |
| AI architect | Designs how the overall AI system fits together | End-to-end system design and component integration (RAG, agents, orchestration) |
| AI security architect | Designs the security controls specific to AI systems | AI threat modeling and the AI security architecture (see [[12. AI Security Controls|Module 12]], [[13. AI Access Controls|Module 13]]) |
| AI governance engineer | Implements governance controls in tooling and workflow | Model registration, documentation, policy-as-code, and control implementation |
| AI risk analyst | Identifies, assesses, and tracks AI risks | The AI risk register, risk assessments, and treatment tracking |
| AI auditor | Independently verifies that AI systems meet policy, standards, and regulation | Independent verification, audit evidence, and compliance reporting |

#### Distinguishing the two classic role clusters

**The build-cluster distractors (data engineer vs. data scientist vs. ML engineer vs. MLOps engineer):**

- Data engineer = data in: plumbing and pipelines. Does not choose the model.
- Data scientist = the analysis and experiments: what to build and whether it works statistically. Rarely owns production.
- ML engineer = the model to production: build, train, serve. Owns the model code, not the surrounding infrastructure.
- MLOps engineer = the lifecycle automation: deploy, monitor, retrain, roll back. Owns the conveyor belt, not the product on it.

**The govern-cluster distractors (AI security architect vs. AI governance engineer vs. AI risk analyst vs. AI auditor):**

- Security architect = designs the controls (technical).
- Governance engineer = implements the governance machinery — policies as code, registers, documentation.
- Risk analyst = assesses and quantifies the risk; owns the risk register, not the controls.
- Auditor = independently verifies; must be separate from whoever built or governs the system.

If a scenario question says "independently verifies compliance," the answer is the AI auditor, not the governance engineer — the governance engineer implements controls, the auditor checks them.

## 4.2 — Risks Associated with AI

Objective 4.2: "Explain risks associated with AI."

### Responsible AI

Responsible AI is the umbrella term for developing and deploying AI in a way that is ethical, safe, and accountable. These principles mirror Microsoft's responsible AI principles and are assessed by characteristics, not definitions — read a scenario and identify which principle is being violated or upheld.

The nine principles, exactly as the objective lists them:

- **Fairness** — the system does not produce unjust bias; it treats people and groups equitably. Tested with fairness metrics across demographics (see [[08. Data and Training Attacks|Module 08]] for how bias enters training).
- **Reliability and safety** — the system performs consistently within its intended purpose, is tested before and during deployment, and fails safely when conditions exceed its design.
- **Transparency** — the organization is open about how the system works, what it is for, and what its limits are. Users know an AI system is involved.
- **Privacy and security** — the data used to train and operate the system is collected, used, stored, and protected appropriately; the system itself is secured against attack. See [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]].
- **Explainability** — a specific output can be explained: why did the model produce this decision for this input?
- **Inclusiveness** — the system is designed to work for everyone: accessible to people with disabilities, usable across languages and contexts, not excluding populations through unrepresentative data.
- **Accountability** — named humans remain answerable for AI decisions; responsibility does not evaporate into "the model did it."
- **Consistency** — results and behavior are repeatable and aligned with the intended purpose across deployments and over time; policies are applied the same way everywhere.
- **Awareness training** — everyone touching AI (users, builders, approvers) is trained on the risks and their responsibilities; training is ongoing, not one-off.

> [!tip] Transparency vs. explainability — a classic exam pair
> **Transparency** is being open about the system: what it does, how it was built, and what its limits are. **Explainability** is being able to account for a *specific output*: why the model produced *this* decision for *this* person. An organization can be transparent ("our résumé screener uses a fine-tuned LLM with these safeguards") while still lacking explainability ("we cannot tell you why it rejected this candidate"). Watch for scenario wording: "disclosed that AI was used / published the model card" = transparency; "explained why this individual decision was made" = explainability.

### AI risks

#### Introduction of bias

- Bias enters through unrepresentative training data, historical bias baked into records, proxy variables that encode protected attributes, and feedback loops that reinforce early skew. Attackers can also deliberately poison training data (see [[08. Data and Training Attacks|Module 08]]).
- Governance response: bias testing as a gate in the model-approval procedure, fairness metrics across affected groups, diverse review teams, and monitoring for drift that amplifies skew over time.
- Exam angle: bias is a fairness problem and a liability problem — biased output in employment or credit scenarios also lands the organization in high-risk regulatory territory (see 4.3).

#### Accidental data leakage

- Models can memorize and regurgitate training data: PII, secrets, internal documents, proprietary code. Overfitted or under-tested models leak most.
- Leakage also happens around the model: prompts and logs sent to third-party services, data exposed in vector stores and RAG indexes, and oversharing in outputs. See [[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]].
- Governance response: data minimization and anonymization in the training pipeline ([[04. Data Processing|Module 04]]), DLP on egress, prompt and log retention rules, and testing for extraction before approval.

#### Reputational loss

- A public AI failure — a chatbot that hallucinated a refund policy, a hiring tool that discriminated, a support bot that was jailbroken into offensive output — damages brand and customer trust faster than the technical fix.
- Reputation risk compounds because AI failures are visible and shareable: screenshots outlive patches.
- Governance response: intended-purpose statements, human review for consequential outputs, disclosure practices, and rehearsed incident response for AI failures.

#### Accuracy and performance of the model

- Models are probabilistic; they hallucinate, they degrade (drift), and their error rates are uneven across populations and input types.
- Acceptable error tolerance depends on the use case: a low-stakes summarization draft can tolerate errors a human reviews; a consequential automated decision cannot.
- Governance response: evaluation reports as an approval gate, drift and performance monitoring in production ([[15. AI Monitoring and Auditing|Module 15]]), defined accuracy thresholds per risk tier, and human-in-the-loop where thresholds cannot be met.

#### Intellectual property (IP)-related risks

- **Training data copyright** — was the organization licensed to use that text, code, or images for training? Scraped or bought data without provenance is a live legal risk.
- **Model outputs** — who owns AI-generated content, and can it be redistributed? Ownership of purely AI-generated works is legally unsettled in many jurisdictions.
- **Ownership and theft** — fine-tuned models and prompts can be trade secrets; model extraction attacks steal the model itself ([[10. Model Extraction and Information Leakage Attacks|Module 10]]).
- **Employee-side IP leakage** — pasting proprietary code into a public chatbot can transfer IP outside the organization instantly.
- Mitigations include provenance records for training data, watermarking outputs to mark AI-generated content ([[04. Data Processing|Module 04]]), license compliance checks, and clauses in vendor contracts about training on customer data.

#### Autonomous systems

- AI agents and autonomous systems act with less human supervision, which introduces loss-of-control risk: the system takes actions its operators did not anticipate, at machine speed, with real-world effects (payments, deletes, sends).
- The operational manifestation is excessive agency — granting an agent more permissions, tools, or autonomy than its task requires (see [[11. Integration and Operational Attacks|Module 11]]).
- Governance response: least-privilege scopes for agents, human approval gates for consequential actions, kill switches, rate limits, sandboxing, and logging of agent actions for after-action review.

### Shadow IT and Shadow AI

- **Shadow AI** is the AI version of shadow IT: employees or business units using unsanctioned AI tools and services outside approved channels — a personal chatbot subscription to draft customer emails, a team pipelining tickets through a free public AI service, a developer pasting proprietary code into an unapproved coding assistant.
- Why it happens: sanctioned tooling is slow to arrive, the approved tools are worse, or nobody told the team what is approved. This is why the hub-and-spoke model above must not become a bottleneck.
- Risks: data leakage to third parties (prompts carrying PII, credentials, source code), no monitoring or audit trail, IP exposure, violation of the organization's own policies and possibly external law (EU AI Act obligations attach to use, not just building), and unvetted accuracy.
- Detection signals: DLP hits on AI-service domains, browser and proxy logs, unknown SaaS expenses in billing, and an AI inventory that does not match observed network traffic.
- Controls:
  - Provide sanctioned AI tools that actually meet the need — the single most effective control.
  - AI acceptable use policy that names approved tools and data-class rules.
  - DLP and egress filtering on known AI service endpoints ([[19. Automating Security Tasks|Module 19]] for automating this detection at scale).
  - Browser controls, allowlists for AI services, and integration of AI discovery into SaaS management.
  - Awareness training and an amnesty path: let teams register the tools they are already using, then triage them into sanction, migrate, or block.

## 4.3 — The Impact of Compliance on Business Use and Development of AI

Objective 4.3: "Summarize the impact of compliance on business use and development of AI."

### European Union (EU) AI Act

The EU AI Act is a **binding law** — an EU regulation, not a guideline. It applies extraterritorially: organizations outside the EU must comply if they place AI systems on the EU market or whose output is used in the EU. It regulates by **risk tier**: the intended purpose and capability of the AI system determine which obligations attach.

- **Unacceptable risk — prohibited.** These practices are banned outright:
  - Social scoring by public or private bodies leading to detrimental treatment.
  - Manipulative or subliminal techniques that distort behavior and cause significant harm.
  - Exploiting vulnerabilities (age, disability, economic situation) to distort behavior.
  - Real-time remote biometric identification by law enforcement in publicly accessible spaces (narrow exceptions only).
  - The compliance obligation is simple and absolute: do not deploy these.
- **High risk — strict obligations.** Representative examples: employment and worker management (recruitment, screening, promotion and termination decisions), creditworthiness and insurance pricing, education (admissions, evaluation), medical devices and AI as a safety component of regulated products, critical infrastructure, law enforcement, and migration/border control.
  - Obligations for providers include: a risk management system across the lifecycle, data governance and quality controls for training data, technical documentation, logging and record-keeping of events, transparency to deployers, design enabling human oversight, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, plus conformity assessment before the system goes to market. Deployers carry their own duties (use per instructions, human oversight, input data relevance, logging, informing affected people).
- **Limited risk — transparency obligations.** Examples: chatbots and virtual assistants, AI-generated or manipulated content (deepfakes), emotion recognition and biometric categorization systems. The obligation is disclosure: users must be told they are interacting with an AI, synthetic content must be labeled, and deepfakes must be disclosed as such.
- **Minimal risk — no specific new obligations.** Most AI today (spam filters, product recommendations, game AI, everyday productivity tools). Voluntary codes of conduct apply; existing law (safety, liability, privacy) still applies as usual.

**General-purpose AI (GPAI) / model-provider obligations:** providers of general-purpose AI models — the large generative foundation models — carry their own duties regardless of what downstream developers build: maintain technical documentation, provide information and instructions to businesses that integrate the model, put in place a policy to comply with copyright law (including respecting rights holders who opt out of text and data mining), and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used to train the model.

The Act **entered into force in 2024 with phased application**: prohibitions and literacy obligations apply first, then GPAI and transparency rules, then most high-risk obligations phase in over the following years. Compliance planning therefore has deadlines attached to a calendar, unlike voluntary frameworks. Non-compliance carries significant financial penalties, and enforcement runs through national authorities.

### OECD AI Principles

- The **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) AI Principles** are intergovernmental, values-based recommendations — **not binding law**. Adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024 to address generative AI, they are adhered to by OECD member and partner countries.
- Five values-based principles:
  - Inclusive growth, sustainable development, and well-being.
  - Human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy.
  - Transparency and explainability.
  - Robustness, security, and safety.
  - Accountability.
- They primarily guide **governments** in shaping national AI policy, and they gave many countries (and the EU's own approach) a shared vocabulary. Organizations use them as an ethical anchor and alignment reference, not as a checklist with legal force.
- Exam angle: the OECD principles, NIST AI RMF, and ISO standards all overlap heavily with responsible-AI values (4.2). The differentiator in a scenario is *who it binds*: OECD = no one (intergovernmental values); NIST/ISO = the organization that chooses to adopt them; EU AI Act = everyone in scope, like it or not.

### ISO AI standards

The two ISO standards that matter for this exam:

- **ISO/IEC 42001 — AI management systems (AIMS).** An international, **certifiable** standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI management system. It works the way ISO/IEC 27001 does for information security: documented policy, roles, objectives, controls, internal audits, and management review. Third parties can audit and certify an organization against it. A vendor holding ISO/IEC 42001 certification is showing independently verified AI governance.
- **ISO/IEC 23894 — Guidance on risk management for AI.** Guidance (not a certifiable management system) on identifying, analyzing, evaluating, treating, and monitoring AI risk. It aligns with ISO 31000's general risk process and tells the organization *how* to run AI risk management, including within the 42001 management system.
- Distraction to avoid: **42001 = the management system (certifiable); 23894 = the risk methodology (guidance)**. A scenario asking "which certifiable standard demonstrates an AI governance program?" is 42001; one asking "which provides guidance for AI risk assessment?" is 23894.

### NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)

- The NIST AI RMF is a **voluntary**, risk-based framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, focused on trustworthy and responsible AI. It is **not law** — adopting it does not make an organization "compliant" in a legal sense, though regulators and customers increasingly use it as a benchmark.
- The framework's core is four functions, applied in a continuous cycle:
  - **GOVERN** — cultivate a culture of AI risk management: policies, roles and responsibilities, training, and risk tolerance. (The counterpart of objective 4.1.)
  - **MAP** — establish and document context: who uses the system, for what, where, and what risks and benefits the context creates. Risk identification happens here.
  - **MEASURE** — analyze, benchmark, and monitor: metrics, testing, evaluation, and ongoing measurement of the risks mapped (accuracy, fairness, robustness, explainability).
  - **MANAGE** — allocate resources to prioritized risks: treat, accept, transfer, or avoid; monitor residual risk; respond when risk exceeds tolerance.
- Mnemonic for the cycle: **govern the program, map the context, measure the risks, manage what you found — then keep going.**
- NIST also publishes a companion Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) that maps the framework's controls onto generative-AI-specific risks — useful, but the four function names are what the exam wants.

### Framework comparison

Beta-exam feedback says the tested skill is *applying* frameworks to scenarios, so this table is the heart of the module:

| Framework | Type | Scope | What it asks of an organization |
|---|---|---|---|
| EU AI Act | Binding law (EU regulation, extraterritorial) | AI systems placed on the EU market or whose output is used in the EU | Classify each system by risk tier; ban unacceptable practices; meet tier obligations (risk management, logging, human oversight, transparency); GPAI providers document models and training data |
| OECD AI Principles | Voluntary intergovernmental principles (values-based) | National AI policy; adhered to by OECD member and partner countries | Align AI strategy and use with the five values — no legal force, no audit, no certification |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | Voluntary international standard (certifiable) | Organization-wide AI management system (AIMS) | Establish, maintain, and continually improve a documented AIMS — policy, roles, controls, audits; can be third-party certified |
| ISO/IEC 23894 | Voluntary international standard (guidance) | AI risk management | Identify, analyze, evaluate, treat, and monitor AI risks using an ISO 31000-aligned process |
| NIST AI RMF | Voluntary US framework | AI risk management across the lifecycle | Apply the four functions — GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE — and document risk decisions |
| Corporate AI policies | Binding internally (organizational rules) | The organization's own people, tools, and data | Define sanctioned use, model approval, data handling; enforced through management, HR, and security controls |

> [!tip] Binding vs. voluntary — the beta-exam trap
> A scenario will offer choices like "adopt NIST AI RMF to comply with the regulation" or "get ISO certified to meet the EU AI Act." Neither is true: **only the EU AI Act is law**. NIST, ISO, and OECD are adopted by choice — they can *help demonstrate* diligence (a legal defense argument) but they do not substitute for legal compliance. Conversely, in a purely internal scenario ("which governs an employee's choice of chatbot?"), the binding answer is the organization's own corporate policy, not any external framework.

### Corporate policies

- **Sanctioned vs. unsanctioned** — the organization's approved-tool catalog versus everything else. Sanctioned tools have been risk-assessed, contracted, and integrated with logging; unsanctioned use is Shadow AI (see 4.2). The policy must name the tools, the data classes allowed, and the consequences.
- **Private vs. public models:**
  - **Private models** — in-house or self-hosted (including open-weight models run on the organization's own infrastructure). Maximum control over training data, logging, and access; data never leaves the boundary; higher operational cost and required expertise.
  - **Public models** — third-party services consumed over an API or SaaS (vendor-hosted). Fast, cheap to start, continuously improved by the vendor; but data leaves the organization's control, contract terms govern use of that data, and the organization depends on the vendor's security and roadmap.
  - Rule of thumb: sensitivity of data and criticality of the use case push the decision toward private; speed and cost push toward public. A middle option is a private deployment of an open-weight model in a controlled environment.
- **Sensitive data governance** — classify data, then bind each classification to AI-use rules: what may be trained on, what may be sent in prompts to public services, what must be anonymized or synthesized first, retention for prompts and outputs, and provenance/consent records for training data. Encryption and data-protection controls are covered in [[14. Encryption and Data Safety|Module 14]].

### Third-party compliance evaluations

- Most organizations consume AI built by others, so compliance obligations flow down through **contracts and evidence**, not trust.
- What to collect from an AI vendor or supplier:
  - Security and AI questionnaires: how training data is sourced and licensed, whether customer data trains vendor models, log retention, human oversight claims.
  - **SOC 2-style attestation** — the familiar audit-report model applied to AI suppliers; some vendors extend scope with AI-specific criteria.
  - **ISO/IEC 42001 certification** — independently verified AI management system at the vendor.
  - EU AI Act evidence: technical documentation, model cards, and conformity assessment where a high-risk system is in scope.
  - Testing evidence: red-teaming, evaluation, and vulnerability-handling practices.
- Contract clauses that matter: data is not used for vendor training, breach notification timelines, audit rights, model/version change notice, and liability for outputs.
- Process: risk-tier the vendor, collect evidence, verify (not just collect), monitor continuously, and reassess on material change — the same lifecycle logic as the model-approval procedure.

### Data sovereignty

- Data sovereignty is the principle that data is subject to the **laws of the country or jurisdiction where it is stored and processed**. Governments assert legal authority over data within their borders, and some sectors (health, government, finance in some jurisdictions) impose residency or localization requirements.
- Cross-border AI services make this concrete: prompts and documents sent to a public AI API may be processed in another region, and training or fine-tuning may move data across borders. A public model that processes data wherever the vendor chooses can silently break a residency obligation.
- Governance responses:
  - Prefer providers offering in-region endpoints and contractual data-residency guarantees.
  - Use private, in-region deployment of models for data that must stay under domestic jurisdiction.
  - Map data flows for each AI use case: where training data lives, where inference happens, where prompts and logs are stored.
  - Watch transfer restrictions (for example, GDPR's rules on transfers of personal data out of the EU) and government-access differences between jurisdictions.

## Exam Focus

- **Exact objectives — know the verbs:**
  - 4.1 Explain organizational governance structures that support AI (structures — AI Center of Excellence, AI policies and procedures — and ten AI-related roles).
  - 4.2 Explain risks associated with AI (nine responsible-AI principles, six risk categories, and shadow AI).
  - 4.3 Summarize the impact of compliance on business use and development of AI (EU AI Act, OECD, ISO, NIST AI RMF, corporate policies, third-party evaluations, data sovereignty).
- **Compare-and-contrast pairs to master:**
  - EU AI Act (binding law) vs. NIST AI RMF (voluntary framework) vs. OECD (intergovernmental principles) vs. ISO (voluntary, 42001 certifiable / 23894 guidance).
  - Transparency vs. explainability.
  - Data engineer vs. data scientist vs. ML engineer vs. MLOps engineer.
  - AI security architect vs. AI governance engineer vs. AI risk analyst vs. AI auditor.
  - Private vs. public models; sanctioned vs. unsanctioned tools.
  - Policy vs. standard vs. procedure vs. guideline.
- **Classic distractor confusions:**
  - "We adopted NIST AI RMF, so we are legally compliant" — false; only binding law (EU AI Act) makes compliance a legal question.
  - ISO/IEC 42001 (management system, certifiable) offered as the answer to an AI *risk-methodology* question (that is ISO/IEC 23894).
  - OECD principles presented as an EU legal requirement — they are voluntary, values-based, and predate/informed the Act but do not bind anyone.
  - A bias question answered with "transparency" — bias maps to fairness.
  - MLOps engineer offered for building the model (ML engineer) or analyzing the data (data scientist).
  - Shadow AI framed as purely a productivity issue — on the exam it is a data-leakage, IP, and compliance problem.
- **Likely scenario shapes (per beta-exam feedback):**
  - "Which framework applies / which is legally required?" — match the scenario to binding law vs. voluntary adoption.
  - EU AI Act risk-tier classification: given a use case (recruitment screening, chatbot disclosure, social scoring, spam filter), place it in a tier and name the obligation.
  - Responsible-AI principle identification from a short description of behavior or violation.
  - Role ownership: given a task ("set up monitoring and retraining automation," "independently verify compliance evidence"), pick the role.
  - Shadow AI detection and control selection (DLP, sanctioned tooling, acceptable use policy).
  - Private vs. public model selection for sensitive data; data-sovereignty constraints on a cross-border AI service.
  - Third-party evaluation: choosing what evidence to demand from an AI vendor.
- **Recall anchors:** NIST AI RMF four functions = GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE. EU AI Act tiers = unacceptable (banned), high (strict obligations), limited (transparency), minimal (none new). OECD = five values, adopted 2019, updated 2024, not binding. ISO 42001 = certifiable AIMS; 23894 = risk guidance.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| AI governance | The rules, structures, and accountability that direct how an organization builds and uses AI across the lifecycle |
| AI Center of Excellence (CoE) | Central team concentrating AI expertise, standards, review, and reusable components for the organization's AI portfolio |
| Hub-and-spoke model | Governance where a central CoE (hub) sets standards and controls while business units (spokes) build and run their own solutions |
| Policy | Non-negotiable organizational rule stating what is permitted or prohibited, who it applies to, and who is accountable |
| Standard | Specific, testable control statement that converts policy into the minimum bar across teams |
| Procedure | Step-by-step description of how a task is completed so results stay consistent across teams and time |
| Guideline | Recommended practice for cases where a single rigid rule would not fit |
| MLOps engineer | Role that automates the ML lifecycle: deployment pipelines, monitoring, and retraining |

| Responsible AI | Developing and deploying AI consistent with principles such as fairness, reliability, transparency, and accountability |

| Hallucination | Confident model output that is factually wrong or fabricated |

### Answers

1. **B.** The EU AI Act is binding law with extraterritorial reach; recruitment screening is high-risk under the Act. NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 are voluntary — valuable as structure and as evidence of diligence, but they do not satisfy a legal obligation.
2. **C.** Chatbots are the classic limited-risk (transparency) example: users must be told they are interacting with AI. Unacceptable = banned practices; high = strict lifecycle obligations; minimal = no specific new obligations.
3. **B.** The scenario asks *why this specific output was produced* — explainability. Transparency would be disclosing that the system uses AI and what its limits are.
4. **D.** Automating the lifecycle (deploy, monitor, retrain) is the MLOps engineer. Data engineer = pipelines in; ML engineer = build and serve the model; platform engineer = the infrastructure underneath.
5. **B.** Unsanctioned AI tool use outside approved channels is shadow AI; the most effective control is giving teams a sanctioned tool that meets the need (policy and DLP then enforce the boundary). It is not an attack on the model.
6. **C.** Testing, metrics, evaluation, and monitoring are MEASURE. GOVERN sets culture and roles; MAP establishes context and identifies risks; MANAGE treats and monitors prioritized risks.
7. **B.** ISO/IEC 42001 defines a certifiable AI management system (like ISO/IEC 27001 for security); ISO/IEC 23894 is risk-management guidance aligned with ISO 31000 and is not itself a certifiable management system.
8. **B.** Data sovereignty — data subject to the laws of where it is stored/processed — plus residency requirements push toward in-region or private deployment for regulated data; a public API that processes data wherever the vendor chooses would violate it.
