# 17. AI Security Use Cases

> [!info] Exam alignment
> **Domain:** 3.0 — AI-assisted Security (24%)

## Overview

This module covers the use cases half of objective 3.1 — "Given a scenario, use AI-enabled tools to facilitate security tasks." The exam names eleven use cases, and scenario questions test two things about each: what it is and what AI adds, and the caveat — the point at which AI can mislead or fail. Beta-exam feedback flagged "knowing when AI can mislead" as high-value, so every use case below ends with its characteristic failure mode. The single most testable distinction on this list is **pattern recognition vs anomaly detection**: matching known signatures and behaviors of established threats vs flagging deviations from a learned baseline that can surface novel, zero-day threats with no known signature. Expect scenario questions that hand you a threat description (known vs unknown) and ask which approach applies. The tools that deliver these use cases are covered in [[16. AI-Assisted Security Tools|Module 16]], and the automation of them in [[19. Automating Security Tasks|Module 19]].

## Threat Detection and Prevention

Four objective use cases live in this section: signature matching, pattern recognition, anomaly detection, and fraud detection.

### Signature Matching and Pattern Recognition

- **Signature detection** matches known malicious behavior — file hashes, code snippets, traffic patterns, known-bad indicators. It is only effective if you have a signature, and it requires keeping signatures current as threats evolve.
- **AI can detect signatures** — applying signature matching at scale, including fuzzy or behavioral matching that tolerates small changes to known malware.
- **AI can create signatures** — analyzing new malware samples and threat data to generate new signatures faster than manual analyst work, shortening the window between threat discovery and detection coverage.
- **Pattern recognition** is the umbrella term for matching known signatures and behaviors of established threats. It is fundamentally retrospective: it recognizes what it has already seen or been taught.

### Anomaly Detection

- Anomaly detection learns what normal looks like (the baseline) and then flags activity that falls outside it.
- Its power is against **novel and zero-day threats that have no known signature** — the attack does not need to have been seen before, only to be unusual.
- Classic product categories include UEBA (user and entity behavior analytics) and ML-based network traffic monitoring.
- **Trade-off:** anomaly-based approaches raise false positives — anything legitimately unusual (new employee, new application, infrastructure change) can trip them. Tuning and human review remain important; AI flags, humans confirm.

### The Mandatory Distinction: Pattern Recognition vs Anomaly Detection

| | Pattern recognition / signature matching | Anomaly detection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it matches | Known signatures and behaviors of established threats | Deviations from a learned baseline of normal |
| Detects | Known threats, known-bad indicators | Novel and zero-day threats with no known signature |
| Blind spot | Anything without a pre-existing pattern | Legitimately unusual activity (false positives) |
| Failure mode | Stale signatures miss new variants | Noisy alerts erode trust; tuning and human review needed |
| Typical exam cue | A known hash, sample, or technique is named | Never-seen behavior, no signature, zero-day |

A scenario with a **known indicator** (a specific hash, a known attack technique) points to signature matching/pattern recognition. A scenario with an **unknown, novel, or never-seen behavior** points to anomaly detection.

### The Signature Lifecycle

AI touches every stage of a signature's life, which is why the exam lists signature matching as a use case in its own right:

- **Create** — AI analyzes new malware and threat data to generate signatures faster than manual analyst work.
- **Detect** — AI applies signatures at scale, including fuzzy matching that tolerates small changes to known samples, so simple obfuscation does not evade detection.
- **Maintain** — signatures are only effective if current; AI-assisted threat intelligence feeds the updates, and stale signatures miss new variants.

### Fraud Detection

- Fraud detection builds on the same machinery: AI reviews historical patterns of legitimate and fraudulent activity and adapts to new techniques as fraudsters change tactics — something static rule engines do slowly.
- Advantage: real-time scoring of transactions and sessions against learned behavior, catching fraud patterns that rules miss.
- Caveat: the false-positive trade-off is sharper here — a false positive blocks a legitimate customer transaction. Tuning and human review of flagged cases remain important.
- Typical signals: transaction amount and velocity anomalies, account-takeover behavior (new device, new location, impossible travel), and session and behavioral biometrics that verify the user rather than the data alone.
- Like anomaly detection generally, fraud models must be retrained and tuned as legitimate behavior shifts (holiday spikes, new payment methods), or false positives climb.

### Detection at Scale

Security information comes in many forms and volumes. AI helps recognize security-relevant information and trends across large datasets — surfacing the meaningful signal in telemetry that no analyst could read exhaustively, such as a slow rise in failed authentications across the estate that no single log makes obvious.

**Detection vs prevention:** the objective's use cases are detection-centric — recognizing and surfacing threats. Prevention is what happens when a detection triggers an automated blocking or containment action. Keep the two straight on scenario questions: AI recognizing a pattern is detection; AI (or a playbook it triggers) blocking the source is prevention.

## Secure Code Development

- **Code quality and linting:** linting scans source code for issues (code smells and poor practices). AI-assisted linting enhances this by learning the expected syntax and conventions for the team and identifying risky patterns, not just style violations.
- **Vulnerability analysis:** AI turns raw findings into actionable plans — informing prioritization, highlighting likely false positives, and creating recommendations that go directly to developers with suggested fixes.
- **Threat modeling:** AI assists by highlighting attack patterns and introducing threat intelligence — suggesting likely threat scenarios for a system's design that the team may not have considered.
- **SAST and software composition analysis (SCA)** can be augmented by AI to reduce noise and suggest fixes: static analysis finds the candidate issues, AI interprets what the code is actually doing and suppresses the noise.
- **What AI-assisted review flags in practice:** injection-prone string handling, unsafe deserialization, weak or deprecated cryptography, unvalidated redirects, and hard-coded credentials — the same classes a human reviewer hunts for.
- **AI-assisted threat modeling output:** an enumerated attack surface, suggested threats against it (often in STRIDE-style categories — spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege), and mapped mitigations. The team validates the output; AI does not know the business context.
- **Caveat:** AI-generated code and suggestions must still be reviewed — models can introduce insecure patterns, hallucinate vulnerabilities, or propose fixes that do not hold up. AI informs developer judgment; it does not replace review.

## Automated Penetration Testing

- AI can automate some penetration testing tools and techniques — running recon, scanning, and enumeration with less manual effort.
- It assists with reconnaissance, scanning, and identifying likely attack paths through findings, prioritizing what a skilled tester should probe next.
- It can help generate and adapt exploit payloads, and craft social engineering pretexts for authorized phishing simulations.
- It speeds up reporting by summarizing findings and suggesting remediation, often the slowest part of an engagement.
- **Caveat:** human oversight remains essential — AI augments but does not replace a skilled tester. Authorization and scoping rules are unchanged by automation: an AI-driven test still needs the same rules of engagement, and its findings still need tester validation before they reach a report.
- AI is strongest at the mechanical phases (recon, enumeration, report drafting) and weakest at creative exploitation chains and judgment calls — precisely why human oversight is the control, not a courtesy.
- Attackers use the same techniques from the other side; see [[18. AI-Enabled Attacks|Module 18]].

## Incident Response and Incident Management

Incident **response** is the handling of an incident (detect, contain, eradicate, recover); incident **management** is the broader coordination — tracking, prioritizing, communicating, and closing out. The objective lists "incident management" and AI helps across both:

- Organizing information — structuring scattered indicators, logs, and artifacts into a coherent picture.
- Collecting alerts and grouping related events — clustering what is really one incident out of hundreds of alert records.
- Suggesting actions and automating responses — recommending next containment steps, and executing playbook steps directly.
- Summarizing timelines — reconstructing what happened and when for the incident record.
- Drafting communications — status updates, stakeholder notifications, post-incident reports.
- Reducing analyst fatigue by triaging and prioritizing high volumes of alerts — the highest-volume, lowest-judgment work goes to AI first.
- **Caveat:** automated actions should follow approved playbooks, with human oversight for high-impact steps (isolation, blocking, deletion). AI can misjudge severity or group unrelated events; humans stay accountable for consequential moves. See [[07. Human-Centric AI Design|Module 07]].
- SOAR platforms (security orchestration, automation, and response) are typically where these approved playbooks live: AI assists the triage and enrichment steps, and the playbook gates which actions run automatically.
- Mapped to the incident lifecycle — detection and analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, and post-incident review — AI contributes most to detection/analysis (grouping, timelines) and post-incident review (drafting the lessons-learned report), while containment and eradication keep human oversight for high-impact steps.
- Ticket-lifecycle automation of these same capabilities is covered under objective 3.3 in [[19. Automating Security Tasks|Module 19]].

## Language-Driven Security Operations: Translation and Summarization

- **Translation:** multinational operations involve threat intelligence, vendor advisories, attack chatter, and incident artifacts in many languages. AI-powered translation tools aid comprehension across them.
- **Natural-language interfaces** let analysts query logs and data without specialist syntax, and can generate detection rules, queries, and reports from plain-language prompts — the analyst describes the behavior, AI writes the query.
- **Summarization:** compressing long timelines, log dumps, and reports into briefings for stakeholders; AI pulls the key points out of volumes no human has time to read.
- **Caveat (testable):** verify AI translations and outputs — errors can obscure or misrepresent security-relevant detail. A mistranslated vulnerability description, an invented timeline fact, or a dropped severity qualifier can propagate into decisions. Summaries can drop the critical exception or hallucinate a detail that was never in the source.
- Summaries inherit their sources: models compress what is present. If the source omits a detail, no summary recovers it — verify against the original for anything consequential.

| Vulnerability analysis | SAST/SCA augmented with AI triage | Hallucinated vulnerabilities or false-positive noise; verify before acting |

## When AI Misleads: Cross-Cutting Caveats

Beta-exam feedback flagged "knowing when AI can mislead" as high-value. Five failure modes recur across every use case above:

| Failure mode | What it looks like | Guard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hallucinated content | An invented IOC, a vulnerability that is not real, a remediation step no vendor recommends | Verify against authoritative sources before acting |
| Dropped context | A summary or translation losing the critical qualifier ("except when...") or the one anomalous record | Compare output to the source for anything consequential |
| Confident misclassification | Wrong severity, wrong grouping of related events, a wrong translation read as authoritative | Human review of anything that changes decisions |
| Automation bias | Analysts stop checking output because it is usually right | Keep review mandatory; spot-check and track AI error rates |
| Staleness | Pattern and signature approaches that no longer match evolved threats | Keep signatures current; retrain baselines as normal changes |

The exam pattern: a scenario describes AI output that looks right and asks for the next step. The right answer is almost always a verification or human-review step, not blind action.

## Exam Focus

- **Exact objective wording:** "Given a scenario, use AI-enabled tools to facilitate security tasks." Use cases list: signature matching; code quality and linting; vulnerability analysis; automated penetration testing; anomaly detection; pattern recognition; incident management; threat modeling; fraud detection; translation; summarization.
- **Compare and contrast pairs to master:**
  - Pattern recognition vs anomaly detection (known-bad matching vs baseline deviation; established threats vs novel/zero-day).
  - Signature detection vs signature creation (AI applying existing signatures vs AI generating new ones from analyzed threats).
  - SAST vs SCA (issues in your own source code vs risks from external components — see [[19. Automating Security Tasks|Module 19]]).
  - Incident response vs incident management (handling the incident vs coordinating tracking, prioritizing, communicating).
  - Translation vs summarization (comprehension across languages vs compression of volume).
- **Classic distractor confusions:**
  - "Anomaly detection requires a known signature" (that is signature matching/pattern recognition).
  - "Pattern recognition catches zero-day threats" (only anomaly detection surfaces threats with no known signature).
  - "AI pentesting replaces the skilled tester" (human oversight remains essential).
  - "AI-generated detection rules can be deployed without review" (errors can obscure or misrepresent security-relevant detail).
  - "AI linting makes human code review unnecessary" (AI-generated suggestions can introduce insecure patterns).
  - "UEBA is signature matching" (it is anomaly detection over user and system behavior).
- **Likely scenario shapes:** a threat description (known hash vs never-seen behavior) and a choice of approach; an alert-flood scenario where AI triage/prioritization is the right use; a multilingual threat-intel scenario needing translation plus a verification step; a "which use case is AI best/worst suited to" judgment call; an executive-briefing scenario where summarization is right but verification is the tested control.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Signature matching | Detecting threats by matching known malicious behavior — hashes, code snippets, traffic patterns |
| Pattern recognition | Matching known signatures and behaviors of established threats |
| Anomaly detection | Learning a baseline of normal activity and flagging deviations from it |
| Prevention | Blocking or containing a threat once detected — typically via an automated action the detection triggers |
| Baseline | The learned model of normal behavior against which anomalies are judged |
| Zero-day | A threat with no known signature or patch — the classic case for anomaly detection |
| Fraud detection | AI review of historical patterns, adapting to new techniques, scoring activity for fraud in real time |
| False positive | Legitimate activity flagged as malicious — the core trade-off of anomaly-based approaches |
| Code smell | A surface pattern in source code suggesting deeper issues; the target of linting |
| Linting | Scanning source code for issues and poor practices; AI-assisted linting also learns team conventions and risky patterns |
| SAST | Static application security testing — analyzes source code for vulnerabilities |
| SCA | Software composition analysis — identifies external components in code and their risks |
| Vulnerability analysis | Turning findings into actionable plans — prioritization, false-positive reduction, developer-ready recommendations |
| Threat modeling | Identifying likely attacks against a system; AI highlights attack patterns and introduces threat intelligence |
| Automated penetration testing | AI-assisted recon, scanning, attack-path identification, payload generation, and reporting |
| Incident response | The handling of an incident — detection, containment, eradication, recovery |
| Incident management | Coordinating incidents — tracking, grouping, prioritizing, communicating |
| Alert triage | AI grouping and prioritizing high volumes of alerts to reduce analyst fatigue |
| Translation | AI translation of multilingual threat intelligence and artifacts for comprehension |
| Summarization | AI compression of timelines, logs, and reports into briefings and digests |
| Natural-language interface | Querying logs and security data, or generating rules and reports, from plain-language prompts |
| SOAR | Security orchestration, automation, and response — platforms that host approved response playbooks |
| IOC | Indicator of compromise — an observable (hash, IP, domain) associated with malicious activity |
| UEBA | User and entity behavior analytics — anomaly detection over user and system behavior |
| STRIDE | Threat categorization: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege |
| Fuzzy matching | Signature matching tolerant of small variations, so simple obfuscation does not evade detection |
| Automation bias | Human tendency to over-rely on automated output and stop verifying it |

## Self-Check

1. An organization sees suspicious never-before-observed data-exfiltration behavior in egress traffic. There is no known signature or IOC. Which approach is best suited to detect it?
   A. Signature matching
   B. Pattern recognition against known-bad indicators
   C. Anomaly detection against a learned baseline
   D. Manual log review of all traffic
2. A known malware sample has a published hash. Which detection approach applies?
   A. Anomaly detection
   B. Signature matching
   C. UEBA baseline deviation
   D. Threat modeling
3. A security team's static analysis tool floods developers with findings. Which AI use case most directly reduces the noise and produces developer-ready recommendations?
   A. Anomaly detection
   B. AI-augmented vulnerability analysis and linting
   C. Automated penetration testing
   D. Translation
4. A penetration tester uses AI to run reconnaissance and draft exploit payloads. Which statement is correct?
   A. The engagement no longer needs defined scope and authorization
   B. Findings can go straight into the report without tester validation
   C. AI augments but does not replace the skilled tester; oversight remains essential
   D. Social engineering pretexts are out of scope for AI assistance
5. A SOC receives thousands of alerts daily and analysts are fatigued. Which use case directly addresses this?
   A. AI alert triage and prioritization (incident management)
   B. Signature creation
   C. Threat modeling
   D. Code linting
6. A multinational team must understand a threat advisory published in a foreign language. Which use case applies, and what must the team do?
   A. Summarization; nothing further
   B. Translation; verify the output, since errors can misrepresent security-relevant detail
   C. Translation; treat the output as authoritative
   D. Anomaly detection; retrain the model
7. Which use case has AI generating new detection content from newly analyzed malware?
   A. Anomaly detection
   B. Signature creation
   C. Fraud detection
   D. Regression testing
8. An AI-based UEBA system keeps flagging a legitimate new backup service as malicious. What is the correct operational response?
   A. Remove the service from production
   B. Disable anomaly detection entirely
   C. Tune the baseline and keep human review of flagged activity
   D. Switch to signature-only detection

### Answers

1. **C** — No known signature exists, so signature/pattern approaches are blind to it; anomaly detection learns normal and flags the deviation, catching novel and zero-day threats.
2. **B** — A published hash of known malware is a signature; signature matching detects it. Pattern recognition is the umbrella, but "known hash" maps to signature matching.
3. **B** — AI vulnerability analysis prioritizes findings and highlights false positives, and AI-assisted linting identifies risky patterns — turning noise into actionable plans for developers.
4. **C** — Automation does not change authorization, and findings need validation. AI speeds recon, payloads, and reporting, but the skilled tester remains in charge.
5. **A** — Grouping related alerts and prioritizing by severity is the incident-management use case that reduces analyst fatigue.
6. **B** — Translation is the use case; the caveat is verification — translation errors can obscure or misrepresent security-relevant detail.
7. **B** — AI can create signatures, not only apply them — shortening the window between new malware and detection coverage.
8. **C** — False positives are the trade-off of anomaly-based approaches; the fix is tuning and human review, not abandoning the approach.
